AI in practice: how it helps me in life, work and projects

For me, AI isn't a magic black box or a replacement for thinking. It's a partner that helps me write, plan, find bugs, build websites, run my smart home and orient myself in complex topics.

How I split the work between ChatGPT, Codex and Claude Code

I use ChatGPT mainly as a partner for thinking through app development — not just for writing a piece of code. I bring it in the moment I have only an idea or a problem. First we work out what I actually want to build and how it should behave — breaking down requirements, finding weak spots in the current solution, sketching architecture. That often turns into a precise working prompt for Claude Code or Codex, which then works directly on the app's source code.

I use Codex specifically for deep audits — mapping the real state of an app before anything changes. Claude Code then does the implementation and fixes directly in the code. Within Claude Code I also pick the model by phase: Fable 5 for audit and deep analysis, Opus 5 for the actual implementation work.

Vibe coding: when I describe an idea in plain language and AI helps with code

Vibe coding means a person describes what they want to build and AI helps design or write the code. It does NOT mean blindly accepting whatever AI generates. In my projects it's important: first the idea, then the design, then the review, then the test, and only then deployment.

I don't treat it as "AI builds the app and I just click". I treat it as collaboration: I set direction, AI proposes solutions, then we test, fix and guard safety. AI helps me turn ideas into code — but decisions, review and final deploy stay with me.
1AI helps turn ideas into code. I don't have to write everything from scratch.
2AI can design structure. Before I start, it's good to see how it could look.
3AI can find bugs in existing code. It often sees what I miss.
4AI can produce a first version quickly. But it's a first version, not final.
5A human must review, test and decide. Without that, vibe coding is chaos.
6For sensitive things (finance, home, data) I insist on a rollback plan. If something breaks, I must be able to revert.

How my apps really come to be

This isn't theory — it's the process I've actually run dozens of times on real changes to my apps and this website. It's not "write a prompt and it's done". It's a loop where AI does a lot of the work independently, but within clear boundaries and gates it must not skip.

1Idea / request. I state what I want and why — the goal, not the technical solution.
2AI maps the real state. It reads the code, config and docs first — it doesn't guess how the app works.
3Audit and gap analysis. What's true, what's stale, what's missing, what's a risk. No fix without an audit.
4Safe implementation plan. What's in scope, what won't change, what the rollback is if something breaks.
5RED. I reproduce the bug with a test that genuinely fails first — otherwise I can't tell if the fix actually fixed this.
6Implementation. The smallest reasonable fix — not a rewrite of everything around it "because it could be nicer".
7PASS. The same test that was RED must now pass.
8Regression. Checking the fix didn't break anything else nearby.
9Runtime test. The app actually runs and the behaviour is verified live, not just in a test.
10Visual / E2E. AI walks the app like a user would — pages, dialogs, mobile and desktop.
11Diagnostics. Checking the console, network requests, performance and accessibility.
12Final report. What changed, why, what was tested, what passed, and what remains a watch item.
13GO / NO-GO. Nothing ships without my explicit approval — never automatically.
This exact process is what a recent audit of this website went through: AI first verified whether the version numbers shown on the site matched reality (they didn't), wrote tests that provably catch that mismatch, fixed the root cause, and ran real browser and accessibility checks before and after the fix — only with that evidence in hand did anything go live.

How a bug on another machine reaches me

When an app crashes or misbehaves on the laptop, I need to find out about it where I actually work — on the mini PC, with AI at hand. The principle is simple:

↓
App diagnosticslog / error message
↓

↓

AI gets the error report, tries to reproduce it on the mini PC, finds the root cause and proposes the smallest safe fix — following the same RED → FIX → PASS process as regular development work. Nothing gets "fixed by guessing" without reproduction.

Why AI often doesn't work for people

It's not always AI's fault. Mostly it's about how you talk to it.

1. They ask too generally

"Write me something about Italy" leads nowhere. AI doesn't know who it's writing for, how long, or what interests you. The more specific the question, the better the answer.

2. They don't give context

AI doesn't know who you are, what you do, who it's for, or what the result should be. A few sentences of context solve most problems.

3. They want a finished result without review

AI can help, but the result needs to be read — especially for finance, health, law, tech and smart homes.

4. They don't specify output format

There's a difference between wanting a table, an article, a prompt, a checklist, a plan or a tech procedure. If you don't say, AI guesses.

5. They don't continue the dialogue

AI works best as a conversation. The first answer is often just the start — a follow-up "now shorten it / focus on X / add examples" massively raises quality.

6. They believe everything

AI can sound confident even when wrong. So facts need verification — especially numbers, dates, names and quotes.

How to ask AI better

I have a simple formula that works for 90% of cases.

Example — web
You are an experienced personal website editor. Help me write a page about traveling to Italy for ordinary readers. The text should be human, practical, free of jargon. Output as a structure of headings, paragraphs and CTA buttons.
Example — smart home
You are a senior smart home architect. First do an audit, change nothing without my GO. Find risks, propose a safe procedure, prepare rollback and only then recommend the next step.

Where I'm careful

AI can't vouch for truth. It doesn't always see the current state of the world unless it has data access. It doesn't know your files unless you give them to it. It can confuse links. For important decisions, human review is needed.

!Vibe coding can create working but unsafe code. First versions often miss edge cases and security.
!AI can overlook security flaws. Especially around auth, encryption and permissions.
!AI can accidentally suggest exposing private data. Tokens, keys, emails, paths — review everything before commit.
!For finance, taxes, health, law and electricity, human review is mandatory. AI is a tool, not a responsible expert.
!For the web I check what goes public. No internal URLs, no tokens, no sensitive screenshots.
The biggest mistake isn't using AI. The biggest mistake is not checking what you let through to the world.
Be especially careful with: finance · health · law · security · electricity · home automation · personal data.

How I know an app actually works

Green tests aren't proof an app is fine. So no change counts as done here until it passes through all of these steps:

REDbug reproduced by a test
→
FIXsmallest safe fix
→
PASSthe test now passes
→
Regressionnothing else broke
→
Runtime + visualactually run it, walk it like a user

Before anything ships, it also has to clear a completion gate — an app is either GO (done and tested), PASS_WITH_WATCH (working, but with a small thing to keep an eye on), or BLOCKED (can't ship until something is resolved). I take the difference between "the implementation is done" and "the app is allowed to ship" seriously — they're two different things.

For apps that crunch numbers (finance, portfolio), one more rule applies: if the app doesn't have enough reliable data for a confident conclusion, it must not make up a result. It should show "unavailable" or "needs review" rather than a made-up number. And totals and subtotals must reconcile mathematically — it's not enough that a result "somehow came out right".

Real data (bank statements, invoices, investment records) is never copied into development or into AI in its final form — apps are built and tested on test/anonymized data, real data stays out of the source code.

What AI does and what I decide

AI works quite independently — but only within limits I set. Decisions with real consequences (deploy, delete, anything irreversible) are always mine.

AI does on its own

  • explores the app's code and documentation
  • proposes the audit and finds discrepancies
  • writes and runs tests (both RED and PASS)
  • implements the fix within the agreed scope
  • walks the app like a user (visual/E2E)
  • writes up what changed and why

I decide

  • the brief and priority — what to fix and why
  • approving the implementation plan and its scope
  • the final GO/NO-GO before deployment
  • what's sensitive and must never leave my machine
  • what becomes public content
  • any touch of production/real data

My approach

I use AI gradually. First the idea, then the design, then the review, then the test. For important things, I want a report, rollback plan and clear confirmation before anything is deployed. Thanks to that, AI doesn't become chaos, it becomes a tool that helps keep order.

I use this approach for smart home, web and finance. Less surprise, more control. AI helps me do each step faster, but I still make the decisions myself.

AI isn't just for programmers. It can help anyone who wants to write better, plan, learn, find errors or organize information. The most important thing isn't knowing all the tools. The most important thing is asking well.
