1. Mafia — basic context

Historical mafia organisations include Cosa Nostra (Sicily), ’Ndrangheta (Calabria), Camorra (Campania) and Sacra Corona Unita (Apulia). Their activities extend beyond these regions.

Menu prices, a restaurant’s location or staff inviting customers inside do not prove mafia involvement. Zero risk cannot be promised to visitors. Check current travel advice and guard your belongings, especially in crowds and public transport.

2. Customs, rules and fines

At the table and greetings

  • Customs are not laws: cappuccino is typical at breakfast, but you can order it later. Cheese is usually omitted from seafood pasta, depending on the recipe. Spaghetti is normally eaten with a fork; mopping up sauce with bread (fare la scarpetta) suits informal settings.
  • Coperto is neither a tip nor the same as servizio. Check the menu for charges and table service. In Lazio, including Rome, charging coperto is prohibited even if listed on the menu. Regional law, Article 77.
  • Tipping is voluntary, with no required percentage. Buongiorno, buonasera and salve are polite greetings; ciao is informal, not forbidden in shops.

Clothing

  • At St Peter’s cover your shoulders; men should wear long trousers, women trousers or skirts below the knee. Check each attraction’s own admission rules. Basilica requirements.
  • Dress before leaving the beach. Swimwear and bare-torso restrictions are local, not a single nationwide rule; check the area and dates covered by the municipal ordinance.

Beaches and islands

  • Sardinia: removing sand, pebbles or shells is prohibited (€500–3,000). Walking on dunes and vehicle access to beaches: €100–1,000. Regional rules.
  • La Pelosa: bookings required from 15 May to 15 October 2026, with 1,500 daily places. Children under 12 need no booking. Use a mat beneath your towel. Check dates, availability and conditions on the official booking website.
  • Capri: do not assume every beach requires a reservation. Check your beach’s rules and current municipal ordinances, especially seasonal traffic restrictions.

Driving

  • ZTL: restrictions depend on zone, hours and vehicle category. Navigation or a hotel booking does not grant entry. Confirm the route and plate-registration procedure with your accommodation beforehand; permission does not automatically cover other zones or reserved lanes. Example: Florence. Penalties depend on the specific offence.
  • Tolls: many motorways use toll booths, but barrier-free roads also exist. On Pedemontana Lombarda, unpaid passages must be paid within the following 15 calendar days. Operator’s payment rules.

Monuments and public spaces

  • Rome: do not bathe in or misuse historic fountains. Respect restrictions on sitting and eating at monuments, including the Spanish Steps. Penalties depend on the offence and applicable rules. Municipal rules.
  • Venice: the city lists €100–200 for prohibited sitting or eating on the ground, bridges, steps and other specified places. Do not attach love locks to bridges or monuments. Prohibitions and penalties.
  • Elsewhere, follow local signs and visitor regulations. Venice’s fines do not automatically apply in Florence or Verona; damaging a monument may have additional legal consequences.

3. Myths — not universal rules

  • Ordering un caffè usually means espresso. Piccolo is not the usual Italian name for this drink.
  • Cappuccino is typical at breakfast, not forbidden after 11 a.m. This is a custom, not a medical rule about milk digestion.
  • Tipping is voluntary. There is no prescribed maximum or mandatory percentage.
  • Italian cooking varies by region and restaurant. Bologna traditionally pairs ragù with tagliatelle, but that does not mean other combinations cannot be found.
  • Bread with olive oil exists in Italy, for example bruschetta or oil tastings. One restaurant’s customs do not represent the whole country.
  • Espresso can be enjoyed standing or seated. Al banco and al tavolo prices can differ; there is no fixed multiplier, so check the price list.

4. Language corner — the minimum that opens doors

Italians always appreciate effort. Even a few greetings in Italian dramatically change your reception.

Greetings and courtesy

  • Buongiorno — good day · Buonasera — good evening
  • Per favore — please · Grazie — thank you · Prego — you're welcome
  • Scusi — excuse me (formal) · Scusa — sorry (informal)
  • Mi dispiace — I'm sorry · Va bene — OK / fine
  • Parla inglese? — Do you speak English?

In the restaurant

  • Il conto, per favore — the bill, please
  • Vorrei... — I'd like...
  • Senza... — without... (allergies, e.g. senza glutine)
  • Acqua naturale / frizzante — still / sparkling water
  • Un caffè — an espresso (just say "un caffè", they know)

Numbers and days

  • 1 uno · 2 due · 3 tre · 4 quattro · 5 cinque · 6 sei · 7 sette · 8 otto · 9 nove · 10 dieci
  • Lunedì (Mon), Martedì, Mercoledì, Giovedì, Venerdì, Sabato, Domenica

5. Coffee types — complete overview

Italian coffee is its own world. Wrong order = polite but knowing smile from the barista. A small guide so you don't stand out as a foreigner.

  • Caffè = espresso. Default. If you just say "un caffè", you get espresso.
  • Cappuccino = espresso + steamed milk + foam. Typically at breakfast, with no ban later in the day.
  • Caffè macchiato = espresso "stained" with a touch of milk. OK any time.
  • Latte macchiato = warm milk "stained" with espresso. Mostly for children/breakfast.
  • Caffè americano = espresso diluted with hot water. Italians drink it rarely; for tourists.
  • Caffè corretto = espresso "corrected" with grappa, sambuca, or other liquor.
  • Caffè freddo / shakerato = iced espresso (summer drink).
  • Caffè decaffeinato = decaf. Just say "decaffeinato".

How to drink: mostly standing at the bar. Pay first at the cash register (cassa), take receipt to barista, drink at the bar. Check the price list: table service may cost more, with no fixed multiplier.

6. Safety — pickpockets, scams, how to protect yourself

Where to be most careful

  • Rome: bus lines around Termini, area around Colosseum, Trevi Fountain.
  • Naples: Spaccanapoli, central station, Piazza Garibaldi area at night.
  • Florence: around Ponte Vecchio and Uffizi.
  • Milan: Duomo area and Central Station.
  • Venice: Rialto, San Marco, water taxis.

Most common scams

  • "Free" bracelet / rose — someone ties a bracelet on or hands you a rose, then demands money. Just say "no grazie" and don't take it.
  • Stolen wallet trick — distraction (someone bumps into you / asks for directions / spills coffee), while accomplice steals wallet.
  • Fake police — "officers" ask to see your wallet "to check for counterfeit bills". Ask for identification. If you suspect impersonation, call 112 to verify; do not hand cash to an unverified person.
  • Inflated restaurant bill — always read the bill before paying. Check servizio against the menu and query items you did not order. Coperto is prohibited in Lazio.
  • Taxi: use licensed taxis from official ranks. Check the official tariff: some airport routes have a regulated fixed fare, so a fixed price is not automatically a scam.

Practical advice

  • Carry money in front pockets or money belt under clothes. Backpack on chest in crowds.
  • Don't leave bag on chair back in cafés/restaurants — keep it on your lap or between feet.
  • Photo copies of documents (passport, insurance) in luggage + phone cloud.
  • Emergency number: 112 (universal EU) or 113 (police).
  • Travel insurance with theft cover — pays off for any longer trip.

Italy is generally a safe country for tourists. The main risk is petty theft and scams, not violence. Precautions reduce risk but cannot eliminate it.
