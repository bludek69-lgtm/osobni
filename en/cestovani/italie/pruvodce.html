1. Mafia — basic context

Sources checked 27 October 2026. Statistics are labelled with the year they cover.

The mafia is not a single organisation. Different criminal structures use intimidation, extortion, corruption and money laundering. Their influence can extend into seemingly ordinary businesses. INTERPOL — organised crime in Italy.

Names worth knowing

  • Cosa Nostra: historically associated with Sicily.
  • ’Ndrangheta: originates in Calabria; its networks also operate outside Italy.
  • Camorra: associated with Campania and Naples, comprising different clans.
  • Sacra Corona Unita and other Apulian groups: Apulia does not have just one organisation. DIA also distinguishes groups from Foggia and Bari; Sacra Corona Unita is associated mainly with Salento.

These are areas of origin and historical activity, not limits to their operations or labels for local residents. DIA — 2024 report, in English (PDF).

How their influence appears

Alongside drug trafficking, activities include extortion, laundering proceeds and infiltrating the economy. Investigations have also documented corruption of officials and interference in elections and public procurement. One example is the 2023 international operation against an ’Ndrangheta network; a specific case cannot automatically be applied to every business in the region. Europol — a case in Calabria.

What the state does

Direzione Investigativa Antimafia (DIA) coordinates investigations into mafia structures. Action against their assets is also important. The report for 2024, presented in May 2025, records 53 completed investigative operations and 309 measures restricting personal liberty. These are not statistics for 2026. Ministry of the Interior — DIA results for 2024.

Prevention also protects public investment from infiltration. In July 2026, the Ministry of the Interior published information on the relevant unit’s activities through 30 June 2026. Ministry of the Interior — prevention in 2026.

What this means for visitors

Menu prices, a restaurant’s appearance or staff greetings do not reveal mafia links. A business or person cannot be labelled this way without evidence. Reports of organised crime are not safety assessments of every street or tourist route.

2. Customs, rules and fines

Checked as of 27 October 2026. Amounts apply to specific places and offences; seasonal rules must be checked for the year of your visit.

At the table and when greeting people

  • Customs are not laws: cappuccino is typical at breakfast, but you can order it later. Cheese is not usually added to fish pasta, though recipes vary. Spaghetti is usually eaten with a fork; in informal settings, using bread to finish the sauce (fare la scarpetta) is fine.
  • Coperto is not a tip and is not the same as servizio. Check surcharges and service charges on the menu. In Lazio, including Rome, charging coperto is prohibited; listing it on the menu does not make it legal. Regional law, Article 77.
  • Tipping is optional, with no mandatory percentage. Buongiorno, buonasera and salve are polite choices; ciao is informal, not forbidden in shops.

Clothing

  • At St Peter’s Basilica, everyone must cover their shoulders. Men should wear long trousers; women should wear trousers or a skirt below the knee. Shorts and miniskirts are not allowed. Check the individual visitor rules at other sites. Basilica rules.
  • Dress when leaving the beach. Restrictions on swimwear or bare torsos are local, not uniform across Italy. The relevant municipal ordinance and its geographical and time limits apply.

Beaches and islands

  • Sardinia: removing sand, pebbles or shells is prohibited (€500–3,000). Walking on dunes or driving onto beaches: €100–1,000. Regional rules.
  • La Pelosa: the official portal for the 2026 season listed bookings from 15 May to 15 October and entry with a QR code. That period has ended. Check dates, prices, children’s rules and required equipment for the next season on the official booking website; the 2026 conditions do not automatically apply to 2027. The portal also warns about fake booking sites.
  • Capri: do not assume every beach requires a booking. Before travelling, check the chosen beach’s rules and current municipal ordinances, particularly seasonal traffic restrictions.

Cars and transport

  • ZTL: restrictions depend on the zone, time and vehicle category. Navigation directions or a hotel booking are not entry permits. Agree the authorised route and plate-registration procedure with your accommodation in advance; this does not automatically cover other zones or reserved lanes. Example: Florence. The penalty depends on the specific offence.
  • Florence — Scudo Verde: an emissions restriction covering a wider area than the historic ZTL. The municipal tourism portal states that, since 1 July 2025, it covers Euro 0 petrol and Euro 0–1 diesel vehicles. From 1 December 2026 an extension is announced for Euro 1 petrol vehicles registered before 1997 and Euro 2 diesels registered before 2001, subject to the city’s exceptions and conditions. For an older car, check its emissions class and any exemption before travelling. City of Florence — ZTL and Scudo Verde.
  • Tolls: many motorways use toll booths, but barrier-free sections also exist. Unpaid journeys on Pedemontana Lombarda must be paid within the following 15 calendar days. Operator’s payment rules.

Monuments and public spaces

  • Rome: do not bathe in historic fountains or otherwise misuse them. Respect restrictions on sitting and eating at monuments, including the Spanish Steps. Penalties follow the specific offence and applicable rules. City rules.
  • Venice: for prohibited sitting, lying down or eating on the ground, bridges, steps and other listed locations, the city specifies 100–200 €. Bathing in canals: 350 €; wearing swimwear or going bare-chested: 250 €. Some offences also carry an order to leave the location immediately. These are not uniform fines across Italy. City list of restrictions and penalties.
  • Elsewhere, follow the city or museum’s visitor rules and signs. Do not apply Venice’s fine amounts to Florence or Verona; damaging a monument can have additional legal consequences.

3. Myths — not universal rules

  • Ordering un caffè usually means espresso. Piccolo is not the usual Italian name for this drink.
  • Cappuccino is typical at breakfast, not forbidden after 11 a.m. This is a custom, not a medical rule about milk digestion.
  • Tipping is voluntary. There is no prescribed maximum or mandatory percentage.
  • Italian cooking varies by region and restaurant. Bologna traditionally pairs ragù with tagliatelle, but that does not mean other combinations cannot be found.
  • Bread with olive oil exists in Italy, for example bruschetta or oil tastings. One restaurant’s customs do not represent the whole country.
  • Espresso can be enjoyed standing or seated. Al banco and al tavolo prices can differ; there is no fixed multiplier, so check the price list.

4. Language corner — the minimum that opens doors

Italians always appreciate effort. Even a few greetings in Italian dramatically change your reception.

Greetings and courtesy

  • Buongiorno — good day · Buonasera — good evening
  • Per favore — please · Grazie — thank you · Prego — you're welcome
  • Scusi — excuse me (formal) · Scusa — sorry (informal)
  • Mi dispiace — I'm sorry · Va bene — OK / fine
  • Parla inglese? — Do you speak English?

In the restaurant

  • Il conto, per favore — the bill, please
  • Vorrei... — I'd like...
  • Senza... — without... (allergies, e.g. senza glutine)
  • Acqua naturale / frizzante — still / sparkling water
  • Un caffè — an espresso (just say "un caffè", they know)

Numbers and days

  • 1 uno · 2 due · 3 tre · 4 quattro · 5 cinque · 6 sei · 7 sette · 8 otto · 9 nove · 10 dieci
  • Lunedì (Mon), Martedì, Mercoledì, Giovedì, Venerdì, Sabato, Domenica

5. Coffee types — complete overview

Italian coffee is its own world. Wrong order = polite but knowing smile from the barista. A small guide so you don't stand out as a foreigner.

  • Caffè = espresso. Default. If you just say "un caffè", you get espresso.
  • Cappuccino = espresso + steamed milk + foam. Typically at breakfast, with no ban later in the day.
  • Caffè macchiato = espresso "stained" with a touch of milk. OK any time.
  • Latte macchiato = warm milk "stained" with espresso. Mostly for children/breakfast.
  • Caffè americano = espresso diluted with hot water. Italians drink it rarely; for tourists.
  • Caffè corretto = espresso "corrected" with grappa, sambuca, or other liquor.
  • Caffè freddo / shakerato = iced espresso (summer drink).
  • Caffè decaffeinato = decaf. Just say "decaffeinato".

How to drink: mostly standing at the bar. Pay first at the cash register (cassa), take receipt to barista, drink at the bar. Check the price list: table service may cost more, with no fixed multiplier.

6. Safety — pickpockets, scams, how to protect yourself

Where to be most careful

  • Rome: bus lines around Termini, area around Colosseum, Trevi Fountain.
  • Naples: Spaccanapoli, central station, Piazza Garibaldi area at night.
  • Florence: around Ponte Vecchio and Uffizi.
  • Milan: Duomo area and Central Station.
  • Venice: Rialto, San Marco, water taxis.

Most common scams

  • "Free" bracelet / rose — someone ties a bracelet on or hands you a rose, then demands money. Just say "no grazie" and don't take it.
  • Stolen wallet trick — distraction (someone bumps into you / asks for directions / spills coffee), while accomplice steals wallet.
  • Fake police — "officers" ask to see your wallet "to check for counterfeit bills". Ask for identification. If you suspect impersonation, call 112 to verify; do not hand cash to an unverified person.
  • Inflated restaurant bill — always read the bill before paying. Check servizio against the menu and query items you did not order. Coperto is prohibited in Lazio.
  • Taxi: use licensed taxis from official ranks. Check the official tariff: some airport routes have a regulated fixed fare, so a fixed price is not automatically a scam.

Practical advice

  • Carry money in front pockets or money belt under clothes. Backpack on chest in crowds.
  • Don't leave bag on chair back in cafés/restaurants — keep it on your lap or between feet.
  • Photo copies of documents (passport, insurance) in luggage + phone cloud.
  • Emergency number: 112 (universal EU) or 113 (police).
  • Travel insurance with theft cover — pays off for any longer trip.

Italy is generally a safe country for tourists. The main risk is petty theft and scams, not violence. Precautions reduce risk but cannot eliminate it.
