Italy isn't one cuisine

Italian cooking is strongly regional. Carbonara from Rome, ragù from Bologna, pesto from Genoa or risotto from Milan — each region has its own ingredients, rules and family variations. The goal of this page isn't to claim the one truth, but to show an honest foundation.

📌 Note

Italian cuisine may vary by region, town or family tradition. I present recipes as a verified traditional version from a specific source, not as the only existing possibility.

Where I draw from

  • Personal experience — from when I lived in Italy (Lerici, Tuscany), from everyday life with Italians and from traveling through different regions.
  • Verified Italian sources — Italia.it, Accademia Italiana della Cucina, official regional tourism sites, DOP/IGP/STG consortia.
  • Italian YouTube cooking channels — GialloZafferano and CookAround as audiovisual supplement, not as sole proof of tradition.
  • Regional differences — where a recipe exists in multiple variants, it's clearly labeled.

Spaghetti alla carbonara · Lazio / Roma

Spaghetti alla carbonara

🇮🇹 Lazioclassic versionhome version

A Roman classic: spaghetti in a creamy sauce of egg yolks, pecorino and rendered guanciale fat. No cream — pasta water helps bring the sauce together.

Ingredients · 2 servings

Servings

Quantities from the GialloZafferano video. Tick off ingredients as you prepare them.

Start cooking
  1. Cut the guanciale into strips and grate the pecorino. Bring water to the boil; salt lightly, as the pork and cheese are salty.
  2. Cook the guanciale in a dry pan over medium heat until golden and the fat has rendered. Lift out the pieces, leaving the fat in the pan.
  3. Cook the spaghetti al dente according to the packet. Reserve a cup of cooking water before draining.
  4. Mix the yolks, pecorino and freshly ground pepper in a bowl.
  5. Toss the pasta in the rendered fat. Remove the pan from the heat, let it cool briefly, then stir in the yolk mixture. Add reserved water a spoonful at a time until creamy.
  6. Return the guanciale, toss and serve immediately. Do not boil the sauce after adding the yolks, or the eggs will scramble.

Regional note: the traditional Roman version uses no onion or garlic. Northern variants sometimes include pancetta but in Rome that's considered a swap.

Cook and practise listening to Italian: listen for tuorli (yolks), mescolare (to stir) and a fuoco spento (with the heat off).

Spaghetti all'amatriciana · Lazio / Amatrice

Spaghetti all'amatriciana

🇮🇹 Lazioclassic versionhome version

An Amatrice speciality with guanciale, tomatoes and pecorino, without onion or garlic. This is the GialloZafferano video version with white wine and chilli.

Ingredients · 2 servings

Servings

Quantities from the GialloZafferano video. Tick off ingredients as you prepare them.

Start cooking
  1. Cut the guanciale into strips, grate the pecorino and crush the peeled tomatoes. Prepare the chilli; remove its seeds for a milder sauce.
  2. Brown the guanciale over medium heat. Add a little olive oil if needed.
  3. Pour in the white wine and let it reduce briefly. Lift out the guanciale, leaving the fat and juices in the pan.
  4. Add the tomatoes and chilli. Simmer for about 10–15 minutes until thickened. Salt to taste, remembering that the cheese and pork are salty.
  5. Cook the spaghetti al dente according to the packet. Reserve a cup of cooking water before draining. Return the guanciale to the sauce and toss in the pasta.
  6. Remove from the heat, add the pecorino and toss. Loosen with reserved pasta water, a spoonful at a time, if needed. Serve immediately.

Guanciale provides both flavour and fat. Substituting pancetta changes the flavour.

📖 GialloZafferano · verify recipe ▶ Video recipe · GialloZafferano

Cook and practise listening to Italian: listen for pomodori pelati (peeled tomatoes), peperoncino (chilli) and sfumare (deglaze with wine and let it reduce).

Tagliatelle al ragù alla bolognese · Emilia-Romagna / Bologna

Tagliatelle al ragù

🇮🇹 Emilia-Romagnaclassic versionhome version

Wide egg tagliatelle with slow-cooked meat ragù. This version follows the GialloZafferano video with pancetta and red wine; pasta quantities are added for a complete dish.

Ingredients · 2 servings

Servings

Ragù based on the video, with 80 g dried tagliatelle per person added. Measure wine in ml. Allow at least 2 hours for simmering. Tick off prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Finely chop the carrot, celery and onion. Cut the pancetta into small pieces.
  2. Cook the pancetta in the oil, add the vegetables and soften over low heat. Add the beef and brown it, breaking up any lumps.
  3. Add the red wine and let it reduce. Stir in the passata and a little hot stock.
  4. Simmer gently for at least 2 hours, stirring occasionally. Add a little stock if it dries out. Finish with a thick sauce; season with salt and pepper.
  5. Cook the tagliatelle in salted water according to the packet. Reserve a cup of cooking water before draining.
  6. Toss the tagliatelle with the ragù. If too thick, loosen with pasta water a spoonful at a time and serve immediately.

You can also serve the ragù with pappardelle. These quantities are for dried tagliatelle; fresh pasta needs different portion sizes and cooking times.

Italian listening: tritare (chop), rosolare (brown), a fuoco basso (over low heat). The video makes the ragù, not the pasta itself.

Gnocchi al pesto alla genovese · Liguria / Genova

Gnocchi al pesto

🇮🇹 Liguriaclassic versionhome version

Potato gnocchi with basil pesto, pine nuts and two cheeses. The video shows how to make the pesto; here we pair it with ready-made gnocchi.

Ingredients · 2 servings

Servings

Pesto proportions from the video, with 200 g ready-made gnocchi per person added. For 2 servings, use half the video's batch of pesto. Tick off prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Rinse the basil gently and dry thoroughly without crushing it. Grate both cheeses and peel the garlic.
  2. Crush the garlic with salt in a mortar, then work in the basil and pine nuts. Gradually mix in the cheeses and oil. If using a blender, pulse briefly to keep the pesto cool.
  3. Cook the gnocchi in salted water according to the packet. Reserve a little cooking water before lifting them out.
  4. Put the pesto in a bowl and loosen with a little lukewarm cooking water. Fold in the gnocchi and serve immediately. Do not boil or fry the pesto.

Keep the pesto cool. Use short pulses in a blender; traditionally the ingredients are crushed in a mortar.

📖 GialloZafferano · verify recipe ▶ Video recipe · GialloZafferano

Italian listening: basilico (basil), pinoli (pine nuts), mortaio (mortar). The video shows pesto; cook the gnocchi according to the packet.

Risotto alla milanese · Lombardia / Milano

Risotto alla milanese

🇮🇹 Lombardiaclassic versionhome version

Creamy saffron risotto following the Boeucc restaurant version in the video. This version uses meat stock and butter, without added marrow.

Ingredients · 2 servings

Servings

Quantities for the Boeucc version in the video, without added marrow. Butter for the base and for finishing is listed separately. Tick off prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Steep the saffron in a little warm stock for at least 30 minutes. Keep the remaining stock hot. Finely chop the onion.
  2. Soften the onion in the butter for the base. Add the rice and toast briefly, stirring constantly.
  3. Add the wine and let it reduce. Add hot stock a ladle at a time, letting each addition absorb before the next. Stir regularly.
  4. During cooking, add the saffron and its liquid. Cook for about 16–18 minutes, depending on the rice, until al dente. Add stock as needed.
  5. Remove from the heat and stir in the cold finishing butter and parmigiano. Season, rest for 1–2 minutes and loosen with stock if needed; it should spread gently on the plate.

Regional note: in Lombardy served "all'onda" — liquid but not soup. Plated flat on the plate.

📖 GialloZafferano · verify recipe ▶ Video recipe · GialloZafferano

Italian listening: zafferano (saffron), brodo (stock), mantecare (stir in butter and cheese until creamy).

Risotto al pomodoro · all Italy

Risotto al pomodoro

🇮🇹 Italyclassic versionhome version

Simple summer risotto with tomatoes. Not a classified regional specialty but part of home cuisine across Italy. Onion, tomato passata and grana padano.

Ingredients · 2 servings

Servings

Quantities from the GialloZafferano video. Adjust stock to the rice's absorption. Tick off prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Heat the stock and keep it hot. Finely chop the onion and soften in a little olive oil.
  2. Add the rice and toast briefly while stirring. Stir in the passata and a ladle of hot stock.
  3. Cook over low to medium heat, stirring and adding stock a ladle at a time. Taste after about 16–18 minutes, depending on the rice; the grains should be al dente.
  4. Remove from the heat and stir in the cold butter, grana padano and torn basil. Season with salt.
  5. Rest for 1–2 minutes. If too thick, loosen with a little hot stock and serve immediately.

Regional note: variations with mozzarella or pancetta in different regions.

Italian listening: riso (rice), passata (strained tomatoes), tostare (toast).

Spaghetti al pomodoro · all Italy

Spaghetti al pomodoro

🇮🇹 Italymy variationhome version

Simple spaghetti with tomato sauce, basil and olive oil gently flavoured with garlic. Good tomatoes and a gentle simmer make the sauce.

Ingredients · 2 servings

Servings

Quantities from the GialloZafferano video. Tick off prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Peel the garlic and crush the tomatoes. Gently warm the oil with the garlic in a pan; do not let it brown.
  2. Add the tomatoes, season lightly and simmer for about 30 minutes. Stir occasionally and add a little water if needed.
  3. Remove the garlic and add torn basil. Cook the spaghetti al dente according to the packet, reserving a cup of cooking water before draining.
  4. Toss the spaghetti in the sauce. Add pasta water a spoonful at a time if needed, adjust the seasoning and serve immediately.

Regional note: in the south (Campania, Sicily) no cheese. In the north occasionally parmigiano or pecorino added.

Italian listening: aglio (garlic), basilico (basil), scolare (drain).

Penne al pomodoro / arrabbiata · all Italy

Penne al pomodoro

🇮🇹 Italymy variationhome version

Penne with tomato sauce. If with chili, it becomes penne all'arrabbiata (Lazio). Penne (rigate) shape holds the sauce in its grooves.

Ingredients · 2 servings

Servings

Quantities from the all’arrabbiata video. Omit chilli for mild penne al pomodoro, or adjust it to its heat. Tick off prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Crush the drained tomatoes. Peel the garlic, chop the parsley and crumble the chilli if using.
  2. Gently warm a little oil with the garlic and chilli. Do not brown the garlic. Add the tomatoes and salt lightly.
  3. Simmer the sauce for about 12–15 minutes, stirring occasionally. Meanwhile cook the penne al dente and reserve a little cooking water.
  4. Remove the garlic, add the penne and toss. Loosen with a little pasta water if needed. Finish with parsley and serve.

Regional note: arrabbiata means "angry" — because of chili. Roman classic.

📖 GialloZafferano · verify recipe ▶ Video recipe · GialloZafferano

Italian listening: peperoncino (chilli), prezzemolo (parsley), piccante (spicy). The video shows the spicy version.

Penne alla panna · Northern Italy

Penne alla panna

🇮🇹 Northmy variationhome version

Creamy pasta from northern Italy (Lombardy, Piedmont, Veneto). Often with ham (prosciutto cotto) or mushrooms. Watch out: cream in carbonara = mistake, but in penne alla panna = legitimate.

Ingredients · 2 servings

Servings

The version with ham from the GialloZafferano video. Tick off prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Cut the ham into pieces, grate the parmigiano and chop the parsley. Start cooking the penne in salted water according to the packet.
  2. Gently warm the garlic in a little oil, add the ham and cook briefly. Remove the garlic.
  3. Add the cream, pepper and a pinch of nutmeg. Let it thicken briefly over low heat without reducing it too far.
  4. Reserve a cup of pasta water before draining. Toss the al dente penne with the sauce, adding a little reserved water if needed.
  5. Remove from the heat and stir in the parmigiano and parsley. Taste, adjust the salt and serve immediately.

Regional note: this is the north's answer to roman carbonara. Different region, different rules.

Italian listening: panna (cream), prosciutto cotto (cooked ham), noce moscata (nutmeg).

🌾 Italian pasta types and what they suit

13 main shapes you'll encounter. Each is paired with sauces it best holds.

  • Spaghetti — long thin. Tomato sauces, carbonara, aglio e olio.
  • Spaghettoni — thicker spaghetti. Heavier sauces.
  • Linguine — flat ribbons. Seafood, pesto.
  • Fettuccine / Tagliatelle — wide egg ribbons. Ragù, butter sauces.
  • Pappardelle — wide ribbons. Hearty meat ragùs (wild boar, hare).
  • Penne (rigate) — diagonally cut tubes with ridges. Tomato sauces, arrabbiata.
  • Rigatoni — wide ridged tubes. Carbonara north variant, amatriciana.
  • Fusilli — corkscrews. Cold pasta salads, pesto.
  • Farfalle — bow ties. Cream sauces, salads.
  • Orecchiette — "little ears". Apulian classic with broccoli rabe.
  • Bucatini — spaghetti with a hole. Amatriciana.
  • Conchiglie — shells. Holds chunky sauces.
  • Gnocchi — potato dumplings. Pesto, butter and sage, light tomato.

Rule of thumb: thin pasta with light sauces, thick/ridged pasta with heavy sauces.

🌾 How to recognize good pasta and honest ingredients

  • Bronze-die pasta (trafilata al bronzo) — rougher surface holds sauce. Look for "trafilata al bronzo" on the label.
  • Slow drying at low temp — better starch structure. Premium brands list "essiccata lentamente".
  • Italian wheat — "grano italiano" matters for some. Most pasta uses imported wheat (Canada, Australia) — not necessarily worse.
  • DOP/IGP marks — guarantee of origin and method. Examples: Pasta di Gragnano IGP, Pomodoro San Marzano DOP, Pesto Genovese, Parmigiano Reggiano DOP.
  • Olive oil — extra virgin (EVOO), cold pressed, "Italian" not "bottled in Italy". Check origin on label.
  • Cheese: parmigiano reggiano (cow, north), pecorino romano (sheep, Lazio), grana padano (cow, milder). Each has specific use.

⚠️ Common myths about Italian cuisine

  1. Carbonara has cream. No. Egg, pecorino, guanciale fat, pasta water = creaminess.
  2. Spaghetti bolognese is Italian. No. Bolognese ragù goes on tagliatelle. Spaghetti bolognese is a British-American invention.
  3. Pesto must be made in a blender. No. Traditional pesto is made in a marble mortar with wooden pestle. Blender heats the oil and oxidizes the basil.
  4. Italians eat pizza with knife and fork everywhere. Depends. Restaurant pizza, especially Neapolitan, often eaten with hands (folded "a libretto"). Knife/fork in formal settings.
  5. Pineapple on pizza is a global thing. No. In Italy "Hawaiian" pizza is considered an insult to pizza culture. Don't order it there.
  6. Pasta cooked al dente means undercooked. No. Al dente means firm to the bite, not raw. Cooking pasta soft = mushy, sauce won't stick.
  7. Garlic is in every Italian recipe. No. Many classics use no garlic (carbonara, amatriciana, alfredo). South uses more garlic than north.
  8. Parmesan on everything. No. Never on seafood pasta. Never on fish. The fundamental rule of Italian cooking.

🗺 Regional differences

Brief overview of regional cuisine characters:

  • North (Lombardia, Piemonte, Veneto): butter, cream, polenta, risotto, ossobuco, gorgonzola.
  • Center (Toscana, Lazio, Umbria): olive oil, beans, bread soup (ribollita), wild game, pecorino.
  • South (Campania, Calabria, Puglia): tomato base, seafood, mozzarella di bufala, chili, dried pasta.
  • Islands (Sicilia, Sardegna): seafood, citrus, sweet/savory mix (pasta alla Norma, arancini, pane carasau).
  • Mountains (Trentino, Friuli): Austrian/Slovenian influences. Speck, knödel, sauerkraut variants.

The further south, the more tomatoes, olive oil and chili. The further north, the more butter, rice and meat broths.
