Italy is not one cuisine

Italian cuisine is strongly regional. Carbonara from Rome, ragù from Bologna, pesto from Genoa or risotto from Milan — each region has its own ingredients, rules and family variations. The goal of this page is not to assert the only truth, but show an honest basis.

📌 Note

Italian cuisine can vary by region, city or family tradition. I present the recipes here as a verified traditional version according to a specific source, not as the only existing option.

What I draw from

  • Personal experience — from a stay in Italy (Lerici, Liguria), from time spent with Italians and from trips to different regions.
  • Verified Italian sources — Italia.it, Accademia Italiana della Cucina, official regional tourist sites, DOP/IGP/STG consortia.
  • Italian cooking YouTube channels — GialloZafferano and CookAround as an audiovisual supplement for the procedure, not as the only evidence of tradition.
  • Regional differences — where a recipe exists in more than one variant, this is clearly indicated.

Some notes are based on personal experience from Italy. For specific recipes, however, I always provide a verified source, because Italian cuisine is not one universal cookbook, but a set of regional and family traditions.

Spaghetti alla carbonara · Lazio / Roma

Spaghetti alla carbonara

🇮🇹 Lazio classic version home version

A Roman classic: spaghetti in a creamy sauce made from egg yolks, pecorino and guanciale fat. No cream — the consistency will be adjusted by the pasta water.

Raw materials · 2 servings

Number of servings

Quantity by GialloZafferano video. Check off the prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Cut the guanciale into strips and grate the pecorino. Bring the pasta water to a boil; salt it sparingly, both the guanciale and the cheese are salty.
  2. Dry-fry the guanciale over medium heat until it releases its fat and turns golden. Remove the pieces and leave the fat in the pan.
  3. Cook the spaghetti al dente according to the package. Set aside a cup of the pasta water before draining.
  4. In a bowl, mix the egg yolks with pecorino cheese and freshly ground pepper.
  5. Mix the spaghetti with the fat in the pan. Remove the pan from the heat, let it cool briefly and stir in the yolk mixture. Add the reserved water a spoonful at a time until the sauce is creamy.
  6. Return the guanciale, stir and serve immediately. After adding the yolks, do not boil the sauce anymore, so that the eggs do not curdle.

Regional note: the traditional Roman version does not add onion or garlic. You'll occasionally find pancetta in northern versions, but in Rome it's considered a swap.

Spaghetti all'amatriciana · Lazio / Amatrice

Spaghetti all'amatriciana

🇮🇹 Lazio classic version home version

Specialty from Amatrice: guanciale, tomatoes and pecorino, without onion and garlic. Here we cook the variant from the GialloZafferano video with white wine and chilli.

Raw materials · 2 servings

Number of servings

Quantity by GialloZafferano video. Check off the prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Cut the guanciale into strips, grate the pecorino and mash the peeled tomatoes. Prepare the chili; remove the seeds for a milder taste.
  2. Fry the guanciale in a pan over medium heat until golden. Add a little olive oil if needed.
  3. Pour in the white wine and let it boil briefly. Set the guanciale aside, leaving the fat and juice in the pan.
  4. Put the tomatoes and chilies in the pan. Simmer on low heat for about 10-15 minutes until the sauce thickens. Donkey to taste; both cheese and guanciale are salty.
  5. Cook the spaghetti al dente according to the package. Set aside a cup of pasta water before draining. Return the guanciale to the sauce and stir in the spaghetti.
  6. Remove from heat, add pecorino and mix. Add pasta water by spoonfuls as needed. Serve now.

Guanciale will add flavor and fat to the sauce. Pancetta is a substitute that changes the taste.

Cooking and listening to Italian: in the video, try to catch pomodori pelati (peeled tomatoes), peperoncino (chili) and sfumare (pour wine and let it boil).

Tagliatelle al ragù alla bolognese · Emilia-Romagna / Bologna

Tagliatelle al ragù

🇮🇹 Emilia-Romagna classic version home version

Wide egg tagliatelle with slow-simmered meat ragù. This recipe is based on the GialloZafferano video with pancetta and red wine; the amount of pasta is supplemented for the entire plate.

Raw materials · 2 servings

Number of servings

Ragù according to the video; supplemented with 80 g of dried tagliatelle per person. Measure the wine in ml. Expect at least 2 hours of stewing. Check off the prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Finely chop the carrot, celery and onion. Chop the pancetta into small pieces.
  2. Fry the pancetta in oil, add the vegetables and let them soften over a low heat. Add the beef and brown it well; break up lumps with a wooden spoon.
  3. Add red wine and let it boil. Stir in the passata and some of the hot stock.
  4. Simmer slowly for at least 2 hours, stirring occasionally. When the ragù dries up, add a little stock. It should be thick at the end; season with salt and pepper.
  5. Boil the tagliatelle in salted water according to the package. Set aside a cup of pasta water before draining.
  6. Mix tagliatelle with ragù. If the sauce is too thick, add pasta water spoon by spoonful and serve immediately.

Ragù can also be served with pappardelle. This procedure uses dried tagliatelle; fresh pasta has a different amount per serving and cooking time.

Gnocchi al pesto alla genovese · Liguria / Genoa

Gnocchi al pesto

🇮🇹 Liguria classic version home version

Potato gnocchi with basil pesto, pine nuts and two cheeses. The video shows preparation of pesto; here we combine it with ready-made gnocchi.

Raw materials · 2 servings

Number of servings

Pesto ratio according to the video, supplemented with 200 g of finished gnocchi per person. For 2 servings, use half the amount of pesto from the video. Check off the prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Rinse the basil gently and dry thoroughly without wringing. Grate both cheeses and peel the garlic.
  2. Rub the garlic with salt in a mortar, add the basil and crushed pine nuts. Gradually mix in cheeses and oil. When using a mixer, mix in short pulses so that the pesto does not heat up.
  3. Boil the gnocchi in salted water according to the package. Before you take them out, set aside some of the cooking water.
  4. Put the pesto in a bowl and dilute with a little lukewarm water from the cooking. Stir in the gnocchi and serve immediately. Do not cook or fry the pesto anymore.

Prepare the pesto cold. Use short pulses when mixing; traditionally, the raw materials are pounded in a mortar.

Listening to Italian: basilico (basil), pinoli (pine nuts), mortaio (mortar). The video shows the pesto; prepare the gnocchi according to the packaging.

Risotto alla milanese · Lombardy / Milan

Risotto alla milanese

🇮🇹 Lombardy classic version home version

Cream risotto with saffron according to Boeucc restaurant from the embedded video. This variant uses meat stock and butter, no added turkey.

Raw materials · 2 servings

Number of servings

Quantity for the Boeucc variant from the video, without added turkey. The butter for the base and for the finish is listed separately. Check off the prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Let the saffron soak in a little warm broth for at least 30 minutes. Keep the rest of the broth hot. Finely chop the onion.
  2. Saute the onion in the butter intended for the base. Add the rice and fry it briefly, stirring constantly.
  3. Add the wine and let it boil. Ladlefuls add hot stock; the next one only after the previous one has been absorbed. Stir continuously.
  4. During cooking, add saffron with the pickle. Cook for approximately 16-18 minutes depending on the rice, until tender. Add broth as needed.
  5. Set aside, stir in the cold butter to finish and the parmigiano. Season with salt, let rest for 1-2 minutes. Thin with stock as needed; the risotto should flow freely on the plate.

Regional note: in Lombardy "all'onda" is served — liquid, but not soup. It is served flattened on a plate.

Listening to Italian: zafferano (saffron), brodo (broth), mantecare (mix butter and cheese into a cream).

Risotto al pomodoro · all of Italy

Risotto al pomodoro

🇮🇹 Italy classic version home version

Simple summer risotto with tomatoes. Not a classified regional specialty, but part of home cooking across Italy. Onions, pureed tomatoes and grana padano.

Raw materials · 2 servings

Number of servings

Quantity by GialloZafferano video. Add the broth according to the absorbency of the rice. Check off the prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Heat the broth and keep it hot. Finely chop the onion and let it vitrify in a little olive oil.
  2. Add the rice and fry it briefly, stirring constantly. Stir in the passata and add a ladleful of hot stock.
  3. Cook over low to medium heat, stirring and ladlefuls of broth in. After about 16-18 minutes, depending on the rice, taste; the grains should remain on the bite.
  4. Set aside and stir in the cold butter, grana padano and torn basil. Aftertaste of salt.
  5. Let rest for 1-2 minutes. If the risotto is too thick, add a little hot stock and serve immediately.

Regional note: variations with mozzarella or pancetta in different regions.

Listening to Italian: riso (rice), passata (puréed tomatoes), tostare (toast briefly).

Spaghetti al pomodoro · all of Italy

Spaghetti al pomodoro

🇮🇹 Italy my variation home version

Simple spaghetti with tomato sauce, basil and olive oil with a subtle garlic scent. They are the basis quality tomatoes and slow cooking of the sauce.

Raw materials · 2 servings

Number of servings

Quantity by GialloZafferano video. Check off the prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Peel the garlic and mash the tomatoes. Heat the oil with the garlic in a pan over low heat; don't let it turn brown.
  2. Add the tomatoes, lightly salt and let it bubble gently for about 30 minutes. Stir occasionally and add a little water as needed.
  3. Remove the garlic and add the torn basil. Cook the spaghetti al dente according to the package; set aside a cup of cooking water before draining.
  4. Mix the spaghetti in the sauce. Add pasta water spoon by spoonful as needed, season and serve immediately.

Regional note: in the south (Campania, Sicily) without cheese. In the north, parmigiano or pecorino is sometimes added.

Listening to Italian: aglio (garlic), basilico (basil), scolare (mince).

Penne al pomodoro / arrabbiata · all of Italy

Penne al pomodoro

🇮🇹 Italy my variation home version

Penne with tomato sauce. If with chili, it's about penne all'arrabbiata (Lazio). The shape of the penne (rigate) holds the sauce in the folds.

Raw materials · 2 servings

Number of servings

Quantity according to video all'arrabbiata. For a delicate penne al pomodoro, omit the chilli; otherwise adapt it to fervor. Check off the prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Mash the tomatoes. Peel the garlic, chop the parsley and chop any chili.
  2. Gently heat a little oil with garlic and chili in a pan. Do not let the garlic brown. Add the tomatoes and lightly salt.
  3. Simmer the sauce gently for about 12-15 minutes, stirring occasionally. Meanwhile, cook the penne al dente according to the package and set aside some of the cooking water.
  4. Remove the garlic, add the penne and mix. If necessary, thin with a little pasta water. Finish with parsley and serve.

Regional note: arrabbiata means "angry" — because of the chili. A classic in Rome.

Listening to Italian: peperoncino (chilli), prezzemolo (parsley), piccante (hot). The video shows a spicy version.

Penne alla panna · Northern Italy

Penne alla panna

🇮🇹 North my variation home version

Cream paste from the Italian north (Lombardy, Piemonte, Veneto). Often with ham (prosciutto cotto) or mushrooms. Attention: cream in carbonara = wrong, but in penne alla panna = legit.

Raw materials · 2 servings

Number of servings

Variant with ham according to the GialloZafferano video. Check off the prepared ingredients.

Start cooking
  1. Cut the ham into pieces, grate the parmigiano and chop the parsley. Boil the penne in salted water according to the package.
  2. Gently heat the garlic in a little oil, then add the ham and fry briefly. Take out the garlic.
  3. Add cream, pepper and a pinch of nutmeg. Allow to thicken briefly over low heat; don't boil it dry.
  4. Set aside a cup of the cooking water before draining the penne. Toss the al dente pasta with the sauce and add a little of this water as needed.
  5. Set aside, stir in the parmigiano and parsley. Taste, add salt if necessary and serve immediately.

Regional note: southern Italy does not generally use cream pastes. Sever considers it family comfort food.

Listening to Italian: panna (cream), prosciutto cotto (cooked ham), noce moscata (nutmeg).

🌾 Types of Italian pasta and what they go well with

The shape of the pasta is not a decoration — it determines what kind of sauce will "stick" to it. Light oily → long thin. Dense ragù → wide or tubular. Cream → with grooves.

Spaghetti

Long, thin · 1.8–2 mm

All of Italy

A classic. Oil sauces (aglio e olio), tomato, vongole, carbonara.

Vermicelli

Long, very thin · 1.5–1.8 mm

Campania / Sicily

Thinner than spaghetti. Light tomato, seafood, soups.

Capellini / Capelli d'angelo

Very thin "angel hair"

Liguria / Campania

Light broths, delicate oil sauces. Cook for 2–3 min.

Bucatini

Long, hollow inside

Lazio / Roma

Classic partner to amatriciana. The hollow shape holds more sauce.

Drink

Thick hand rolled

Tuscany / Siena

Cacio e pepe, aglio, ragù from the wild. Traditional Tuscan rolled pasta.

Linguine

Long, flat oval

Liguria

Pesto, seafood, fish. "Little tongues" — wider than spaghetti.

Fettuccine

Long, flat, ovate · 6 mm

Lazio / Roma

Roman classics. Fettuccine al burro, alfredo (tourist version), with seafood.

Tagliatelle

Long, flat, ovoid · 6–10 mm

Emilia-Romagna / Bologna

Ragù alla bolognese (NOT spaghetti!). Porcini mushrooms. Tartufo.

Pappardelle

Long, wide, ovoid · 20–30 mm

Tuscany

Game ragù — cinghiale (wild boar), lepre (hare). The deep flavor can carry the wide shape.

Penn

Short, oblique cut

All of Italy

Penne Rigate (grooved) holds better. Arrabbiata, alla vodka, pomodoro, alla panna.

Rigatoni

Short, wide, grooved

Lazio / Campania

Thick meat ragù. Roman preference for carbonara. Grooving is held by sugo.

Mezzi rigatoni

Short, small rigatoni · ½ length

Lazio

A smaller version of rigatoni. Same uses, better mixed in a large pan.

Zites

Long smooth hollow tubes

Campania / Sicily

Traditionally, they are broken by hand before cooking. Wedding Sunday dish with ragù.

Paccheri

Large hollow tubes · 30 mm

Campania

Can be filled with seafood, ragù, cheese. During cooking, it "flattens" into a strip shape.

Maccheroni

Short curved hollow

Apulia / Sicily

"Pasta al ferretto" — a traditional form stretched over a wire. Sugo, ricotta, ragù.

Fusilli

Short, spiral

Campania / Mollis

Cold salad, tomatoes, pesto, ricotta. Spirals hold bits of sauce.

Trophies

Short, hand spun

Liguria / Recco

A classic pair with pesto alla genovese. Most often with potatoes and beans.

Casarecce

Short, "S" shape rolled

Sicily

Pesto alla Trapanese, melanzane (eggplant), ragù di salsiccia.

Busy

Long, twisted

Sicily / Trapani

Traditionally with pesto alla Trapanese (tomatoes, almonds, basil, garlic).

Farfalle

Short, "bow ties"

Emilia-Romagna / Lombardy

Light cream, salad, pesto, salmon sauce.

Conchiglia

Mussels · S/M/L

Campania

Salad, ricotta, seafood. Large conchiglioni are filled (cheese, spinach, ricotta).

Cavatelli

Short, small "shells"

Apulia / Mollise / Basilicata

Traditional with broccoli rabe, beans, ragù. Without eggs — water + semolina.

Orecchiette

"Small ears" · 2–3 cm

Apulia / Bari

A classic from Bari — cima di rapa (broccoli rabe), horsemeat ragù.

Tortellini

Stuffed, ring

Emilia-Romagna / Bologna / Modena

Filled with mortadella, prosciutto crudo, parmigiano. In beef broth.

Ravioli

Filled squares/circles

Liguria / Emilia-Romagna

Ricotta + spinach, pumpkin (Mantova), meat. Burro e salvia, sugo.

Lasagna

Wide baking sheets

Emilia-Romagna / Bologna

Lasagna al forno = ragù alla bolognese + besciamella + parmigiano. NO ricotta (= US adaptation).

Gnocchi

Potato dumplings · ~3 cm

Liguria / Lazio / Veneto

Pesto (Liguria), sugo (Lazio), gorgonzola (north), burro e salvia.

Italian cuisine without shortcuts

🌾 How to recognize good pasta and honest ingredients

There are no mistakes in simple Italian recipes. The quality of the pasta, tomatoes, cheese, oil and proper cooking matter all the more. When a recipe only has a few things, you can't hide them behind ketchup, edamame or a heavy sauce.

What makes pasta quality?

🌾 Semolina di grano duro

High-quality dry pasta is traditionally made from durum wheat semolina (durum). Pasta di Gragnano IGP and Pasta di Gragnano specifications require it as a basic ingredient.

📖 Pasta di Gragnano IGP — discipline

🟫 Trafilata al bronze

Pasta pulled over bronze matrix they have a rougher, duller surface. The roughness catches the sauce better. Smooth Teflon-coated ones aren't bad, but they don't hold sugo as well.

📖 Unione Italiana Food / WeLovePasta

⏳ Essiccazione lenta

Slow drying (24-60 hours at a low temperature of 40-50°C) preserves the gluten structure and flavor. Industrial rapid drying at 90 °C+ "burns" the pasta and worsens the cooking behavior.

📖 Pasta di Gragnano IGP

🟡 Yellow color

The naturally yellowish color comes from durum wheat semolina. Too golden / too white can indicate a lower quality flour or soft wheat mixed in.

📖 PastaItaliani.it

💧 Al dente

"Al dente" does not mean undercooked. It means firm to the bite. The firm texture will help the pasta to combine with the sauce (mantecare) in the pan and not fall apart. The key: 1-2 minutes less than the package says.

📖 GialloZafferano · technique

🧂 Salt water

Italian Rule: 10 g of salt per 1 l of water per 100 g of pasta. Not enough salt water = bland pasta. Salt is added only when the water is boiling.

📖 GialloZafferano

Pasta shape and sauce — why it matters

The shape of the pasta is not a decoration. It determines how the sauce will "grip" the pasta. "Sugo grasso, pasta liscia. Sugo secco, pasta rigata." (Fat sauce — smooth paste. Dry sauce — grooved paste.)

The home version is not a sin

If we adapt a recipe at home according to what we have in the fridge, it's completely normal. It's just good not claim to be a traditional Italian recipe, if we replaced the main raw materials.

That's why I make a distinction on this page traditional recipe (according to an Italian source), home version (self-editing) a inspired by Italy.

⚠️ What is often added at home, but does not belong in the traditional version

The tone is friendly — no cooking police. Just the distinction between "traditional Italian recipe" and "Czech homemade improvisation".

  • Ketchup instead of tomato sauce — ketchup contains sugar, vinegar and spices. It belongs on fries or in a quick homemade improvisation, but not as a base for traditional Italian sugo. Instead: trade wind, pelati San Marzano or fresh tomatoes.
  • Eidam instead of Italian cheeses — edam is perfectly fine in Czech cuisine, but it is not usually a substitute in traditional Italian recipes Pecorino Romano, Parmigiano Reggiano or Grana Padano. These cheeses have a different saltiness, texture and function in the sauce.
  • Cream in carbonara — traditional Roman carbonara has a creaminess from egg yolk, cheese and starchy pasta water. U penne alla panna is the cream legit; not with carbonara.
  • Ham instead of guanciale — u carbonara, Amatricians a Gricia is the guanciale (pork cheek) staple. Pancetta is accepted as a substitute, but ham is not.
  • Ricotta in lasagna instead of besciamella — that's an American adaptation. Traditional Bologna lasagna has besciamella (milk roux).
  • Fried garlic on everything — Roman carbonara, cacio e pepe, amatriciana they don't have garlic. Do not supplement strict recipes "for taste".

Original vs. corresponding raw materials

Expensiveness is not the goal. The goal is the suitability of the raw material for the given recipe. Not all Italian specialties can be found in the Czech Republic — then it is more honest to say "home version inspired by Italy" than to pass off the replaced version as the original.

  • Pomidoro / Passata — quality pomidoro/passata (San Marzano, Pelati italiani) forms the basis of Italian sugo.
  • Olive oil — extra virgin has a different role in Italian cuisine than neutral sunflower. Especially where the oil remains raw (pesto, plate finish).
  • Cheeses — DOP / IGP names (Parmigiano, Grana Padano, Pecorino Romano, Mozzarella di Bufala Campana) indicate a protected production method. "Parmesan type cheese" in the supermarket is often not the same thing.
  • Herbs — basil (fresh!), oregano (dried), parsley. Use sparingly according to the recipe, not a flat rate.
  • Salt — coarse sea salt to the boiling water, fine to the finished sauce.

Summary: Good Italian cuisine is often not about complexity, but about simplicity and the quality of the ingredients. When a recipe only has a few things, you can't hide them behind ketchup, edamame or a heavy sauce. When we edit the recipe in Czech, let's admit that it is home version inspired by Italy — and everyone is happy.

⚠️ Common mistakes in Italian cuisine

It mainly concerns traditional recipes. Nobody forbids home variations — but if you want to avoid looking like a tourist in a restaurant in Rome or Bologna, it pays to know.

1. Carbonara with cream ❌

Traditional Roman carbonara has a creaminess from egg yolk, cheese and starchy pasta water. No cream is added. Nordic homemade version with pancetta + cream = no more carbonara.

Source: Accademia Italiana della Cucina, GialloZafferano

2. Amatriciana with pancetta ⚠️

Traditional amatriciana has guanciale (pig cheeks). Pancetta (smoked belly bacon) is a widespread substitute, but it is not the original. Ham = error.

Source: STG specification EU 2020, Comune di Amatrice

3. "Spaghetti bolognese" does not exist in Bologna ❌

Real ragù alla bolognese is served with Tagliatelle, ne se spaghetti. You won't find the British-American adaptation of "spaghetti bolognese" in Italy — whoever orders it in Bologna will reveal themselves.

Source: Accademia Italiana della Cucina (notarized ragù recipe)

4. Cappuccino after lunch ⚠️

Italians only drink cappuccino until the morning (until about 11:00 a.m.). Milk after a meal is said to disrupt digestion. After lunch only coffee (espresso) or macchiato.

Source: cultural custom, Italia.it tips for tourists

5. Parmesan for fish ❌

Cheese on pasta ai frutti di mare or spaghetti alle vongole is considered a mistake in Italy. The cheese overpowers the subtle fish flavor. Don't put it on pastas with seafood anyway.

Source: Traditional Italian Culinary Practice (Regional Match)

6. Hawaiian pizza is not Italian ❌

Pineapple pizza originated in Canada in 1962 (Greek pizza maker). You won't get it in an authentic Italian pizzeria. In Italy it is considered a joke.

Source: History - Inventor Sam Panopoulos, Ontario, 1962

7. Oil and balsamic for bread ⚠️

In Italy, oil with vinegar for dipping bread does not serve as an appetizer. This is an American custom from Italian restaurants in the US. In Italy you get grissini or bread to eat, not for dipping.

Source: extended anecdote, Italia.it

8. Pasta with garlic in every recipe ❌

Carbonara, cacio e pepe, amatriciana — no garlic. Traditional Roman recipes tend to be austere. Garlic is added where it belongs (aglio e olio, vongole, marinara) — not all at once.

Source: Accademia Italiana della Cucina

🗺 Regional differences

Italian cuisine varies by region, city and family. A quick overview of the main differences:

🇮🇹 North

Lombardy, Veneto, Emilia-Romagna, Piemonte: butter, cream, rice (risotto), polenta, beef, pork, cheeses (parmigiano, gorgonzola).

🌿 Center

Tuscany, Lazio, Umbria, Marche: olive oil, wheat, dried legumes, pecorino, beef, game, simplicity (cucina povera).

🌶 South

Campania, Apulia, Calabria, Sicily: tomatoes, olive oil, fish, seafood, mozzarella, hot pepper, citrus, sugar, almonds.

🏔 Islands

Sicily, Sardinia: Arabic influences (almonds, sugar, spices, citrus), pecorino, wild mushrooms, fish specialties, cous-cous (Trapani).
