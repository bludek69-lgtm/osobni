Places that look like a fairy tale: from Eltz to Alberobello

Luděk · Cestovatel6922. 11. 20264 minutes of reading

Some places in Europe look as if a storyteller had invented them first. Behind their appearance, however, are real people, landscapes and long transformations. The two parts of the series lead from castle towers in a wooded valley to white houses with stone cones in southern Italy.

At every stop it is worth asking what creates the fairy-tale impression: the shape of the land, the light, or the architecture. Sometimes it is old work and trade, elsewhere a ruler’s vision or a film set.

Eltz and Mont-Saint-Michel: a castle in a valley and an abbey above a bay

The residential towers of Eltz Castle rise from a wooded valley. A stream surrounds the rocky spur on three sides, so the buildings grow upwards in a small space. Its complex shape also reflects family history: a division of property in the 13th century created a castle of three co-heirs. A fire in 1920 damaged part of the buildings and roofs. Eltz from 0:22 ↗

Mont-Saint-Michel contrasts small houses below with the tall silhouette of the abbey. Inside, the rhythm changes: the cloister, the Guests’ Hall with Gothic ribs and a large wooden wheel that people turned by walking to haul up supplies. The surviving device dates from the 19th century, when the abbey served as a prison. Mont-Saint-Michel from 1:21 ↗

The Dark Hedges, Neuschwanstein and Bomarzo

The Stuart family planted the beech avenue of the Dark Hedges in the 18th century as an impressive approach to Gracehill House, built around 1775 by James Stuart. Neuschwanstein looks medieval, but Ludwig II meant it as a personal retreat; work on the main palace began in September 1872 and its decoration draws on medieval legends known from Wagner’s operas. Dark Hedges from 2:56 ↗

The Sacro Bosco near Bomarzo was created in the 16th century for Vicino Orsini. Among the trees an elephant carries a tower, the mouth of the Orcus forms the entrance to a stone face, and above the terraces a small temple appears. Bomarzo from 5:30 ↗

Popeye Village, Hallstatt and Goslar

Malta’s Popeye Village was built for the 1980 film Popeye and became an open-air museum after shooting. Hallstatt occupies a narrow strip of shore between the lake and a steep slope; behind the famous view is a real, inhabited town. Goslar combines timber-framed houses, guild traditions and an 11th-century imperial palace. Popeye Village from 6:16 ↗

Skye, Sintra and Reine

Part two begins on the Isle of Skye: the Old Man of Storr, the layers of the Quiraing, Mealt Falls by Kilt Rock and a coast where scientists described Middle Jurassic dinosaur tracks. The museum in Kilmuir recalls island life at the end of the 19th century. Isle of Skye from 0:22 ↗

In Sintra, the two conical chimneys of the royal kitchen define the National Palace; the Fonte Mourisca and Fonte dos Pisões fountains were designed by José da Fonseca in the 1920s and early 1930s. In Reine on the Lofoten Islands, fish dry into long-lasting stockfish between the mountains, and the red rorbu cabins of nearby Hamnøy recall the short path to the sea. Reine from 3:27 ↗

Colmar, Ghent, Rakotzbrücke and Alberobello

Behind Colmar’s picturesque façades are fishermen, tanners and the Koïfhus customs house completed in 1480. Ghent shows its relationship with water on the Graslei quay, at Gravensteen castle and in a former cotton mill that is now an industrial museum. Colmar from 5:14 ↗

The stone arch of the Rakotzbrücke in Kromlau Park, Saxony, probably dates from the 1860s; on still water its reflection almost completes a circle. The journey ends among the white trulli of Alberobello, whose roofs are made of layered limestone slabs. Alberobello from 10:06 ↗

Before you go

All stops are on the maps for the videos. They are in different countries, so treat them as separate destinations and check opening hours, tickets and access with each site. It pays to stop for the details you easily miss in the first panorama.
