AUTO VÝBĚR

🚗AUTO VÝBĚR — used car finder

My own app for finding a used car. It pulls in listings, normalises them, scores them 0–100 deterministically, finds risks and contradictions — and for every car with a published VIN it starts a history check by itself, with no clicking. It runs only on my own PC, sends nothing out and uses no paid database. It can also value my own car and say honestly what that price is based on, and read a VIN from a photo. For a car I am about to go and see, it puts together questions for the seller. From a VIN it pulls the vehicle record from the registry and fills the form with it. 307 automated tests.

What it does

Buying a used car comes down to two questions: is this price sane and what don't I know about this car. Classifieds answer only the first one, and badly — every site holds different fields, the same car sits on three sites with three different odometer readings, and a missing value looks just as innocent as a filled-in one. This app merges all of that and, more importantly, keeps documented and undocumented strictly apart.

  • From a search profile (make, model, year, mileage, price, equipment, location) it finds offers on sources that allow it in robots.txt — for the rest it opens the search in a browser or accepts an import. robots.txt is never worked around and CAPTCHAs are never solved.
  • Every offer is normalised (units, fuel, gearbox, equipment), de-duplicated by VIN and similarity, and compared against the median of comparable cars, including a correction for different mileage.
  • It computes a 0–100 score across six areas where every point shows where it came from. A missing value never raises the score — it is a gap, not a merit.
  • It finds risks: suspiciously low price against the market, an import with no history, a deposit demanded before viewing, contradictions between listings, and well-known weak spots of the model and engine (27 entries, each with a source and a date).
  • For a car with a published VIN it checks the history by itself and feeds the findings into risks, score and watchlist.

Key features

  • Automatic VIN check. The check is queued by itself on every route an offer can enter the app — live connector, AI agent result, pasted URL, CSV/JSON import, and manually filling in a VIN. The queue lives in the database, so it survives a restart; the same VIN in five listings means one check, not five.
  • Honest states instead of reassuring zeros. A timeout, a block or a parser error is never stored as “no record”. And “no finding in the checked sources” says right next to it that this does not mean the car is fine. The time a page was fetched is never passed off as the date of an event.
  • Sources as they really are. Automatic: the app's own listing history, NHTSA vPIC (which it says plainly decodes the VIN but does not provide history) and the Czech vehicle registry — that one has a public, documented API and only needs your own key, which is free. The odometer check, the police stolen-vehicle search and manufacturer recalls sit behind a CAPTCHA or a login, so they stay a manual step with a link and instructions — protections are not worked around. None of it costs anything.
  • Contradictions worth a second look. A falling odometer reading between listings, two concurrent listings of the same car with different mileage, a year or make decoded from the VIN that does not match the ad. Each one states what else it could be — on its own it is not proof of odometer tampering.
  • Pick-lists instead of typing. Make, model, generation, colour and home location are selected (39 makes, 303 models, 79 towns). The model list follows the chosen make, and everywhere there is an “other (type it in)” escape, so the catalogue never blocks an unusual car.
  • Vehicle record from the registry by VIN. The Czech vehicle registry has a public, documented API. The app pulls the date of first registration, the number of owners and operators, MOT validity, withheld documents and the vehicle status — and turns them into contradictions against the ad. For my own car, entering the VIN fills the form by itself. Only empty fields are filled: what you typed stays, and a difference is shown as a difference. Nothing is invented — the registry does not hold mileage, gearbox or drivetrain, and the app says so; the year is labelled as derived from first registration, not from manufacture.
  • Notes for the viewing. The app honestly records what is missing about each car — and turns those gaps into questions for the seller and things to check on the car. Each one says why: a bullet without a reason is just another bullet. It is built only from what the app knows and does not know — missing fields, risk findings, manual VIN-check steps, known weak points of the model. The items are tick-boxes and can be downloaded to print, because you do not take a laptop to a viewing. It is not an inspection or an appraisal and does not replace a garage check; a known weak point of the model is always labelled as exactly that — a place worth looking at more closely on this model, not a fault found on this car.
  • VIN from a photo. Photograph the plate or the stamping and the app offers what it read — saving it is up to you, after comparing with the photo. Recognition runs on my own PC (the OCR built into Windows), the photo is never sent anywhere and nothing is paid for. The task is easier because a VIN has a fixed shape: no letters I, O or Q (the standard left them out precisely because they are confused with 1 and 0) and a check digit in the ninth position. That check digit is not proof though — European makers often do not compute it, so a mismatch does not mean a misread VIN. Most of the work went into stopping the module from inventing a VIN where there is none: a bank account number, an invoice line and “STK 06/2027 KM 182350” are also seventeen characters long, and a random sequence matches the check digit roughly once in eleven tries.
  • Sell my car: valuing my own vehicle. I record my own car and the app works out a suggested asking price, an achievable range and a quick / patient sale variant from the offers it knows. For every compared offer you can see whether it counted and, if not, why — crashed, sold for parts, price conditional on financing, price excluding VAT, a duplicate, or my own listing. No trade-in estimate is issued at all, because the app has no documented trade-in prices, and the basis is asking prices, not documented sale prices — a listing that disappears does not prove a sale. The negotiation discount and the quick-sale discount are stated, adjustable assumptions, not a measurement. The draft advert is built only from recorded data including known faults, and the app never sends it anywhere.
  • Watchlist and alerts. Price and availability tracking, a change history, local alerts on a new risk or a price drop. The app never contacts a seller, never reserves anything and never pays a deposit.
  • Distance estimate without a third-party service. Distance to the seller is computed from an offline table of towns and postcodes; verified against ten known routes with a deviation under 12 %. It is always labelled an estimate, not an exact route.

Status

Beta v0.8.1 (September 2026), 307 automated tests plus a visual check of 20 screens and an interactive walkthrough. Fully standalone — it runs only on my PC in its own window (pywebview), data stays in a local SQLite file, and updates are pulled quietly from my home NAS. Private, with no public hosting and no downloadable installer. It is not an expert appraisal or a vehicle inspection — it is a tool that sorts offers out and, above all, shows what is documented and what is not.

Only partly verified: reading a VIN from a photo has been tried on one real photo of an etched VIN and on rendered plates. One real photo is not a statistic — different light, angle or glare may turn out differently. Two external sources work live: VIN decoding from NHTSA vPIC (not service history) and the Czech vehicle registry, which gives registration and technical data — not service history and not odometer readings. The mapping of the registry's answer is verified on one real car; other cars may have different fields filled in. The odometer check, the police search and manufacturer recalls stay a manual step. The valuation of my own car rests on asking prices, not documented sale prices, which is why it issues no trade-in estimate at all.

📖 Development diary — AUTO VÝBĚR

  • v0.8.1 — the feature was there, it just could not be seen (September 2026) — I reported to myself that a new feature was missing from the app. It was there. The app window had simply kept the old javascript from its cache: after the update a new program was running, but showing the old interface. The static files were served with no header at all saying how long they may be kept — so the browser used its own judgement, kept the old copy and stopped asking. The page itself was covered; the files around it were not. The fix is one line: static files now say always ask, which does not mean download again — only check that the file is current. What is interesting is that no test would have caught this: the server answered correctly, the app worked, the fault was in between. It also explained why a build had failed earlier for no obvious reason — the app was running in the background, holding the files. 307 tests.
  • v0.8.0 — the VIN fills in the form, and what the first live answer showed (September 2026) — with a registry key in place, entering the VIN of my own car fills the form by itself: eleven fields, from make to MOT expiry. Two rules hold it together. Only empty fields are filled — what a person typed stays, and a difference is shown as a difference; the registry is a source, not an authority over what the owner knows about their own car. And nothing is invented: the registry does not hold mileage, gearbox or drivetrain, so the app leaves them empty and says so. The interesting part is what the first real request found. Until then the mapping was written from the sample in the documentation — and two things turned out not to be visible from it. I read the make from a field that in a real answer contains a full stop; the make lives elsewhere, so a dot was printed into the description and a make mismatch against the ad was never checked at all. And I had completely overlooked the field with the vehicle status — on that car it said “DEREGISTERED”. For a buyer that is probably the single most important line in the whole answer. Then I walked the app screen by screen and found four more bugs, two of them untrue statements: a whole column was empty for every source (the interface returns the field under a different name than the UI was reading), and a finding from the registry was labelled “From the ad” — although the data comes from the ministry. No test would have found either; they were all green the whole time. 307 tests.
  • v0.7.0 — “I did not find it” is no proof it is not there (September 2026) — the app claimed the Czech vehicle registry has no public API. That was not true. I got there by guessing interface addresses, getting an error, and turning “I did not find it” into “it does not exist”. It would have been enough to download the page’s own script and read what it calls: the addresses are listed there, along with a link to the ministry’s official documentation. The key is free. It is the same lesson as with reading an etched VIN, from the other side: there, thirty green tests were no proof it worked; here, a few error responses were no proof it did not exist. The module now takes first registration from the registry (and spots an import), the number of owners, MOT validity including expiry, and withheld documents. The key lives in the Windows credential store — not in the database, not in the installer. Without a key the source honestly reports a manual step and says where to get one; it never claims “no record”. The odometer check and the police search have a CAPTCHA, which I do not work around, so they stay manual. 281 tests.
  • v0.6.0 — gaps turned into questions, and a photo that exposed a bug (September 2026) — from the start the app has honestly recorded what is missing about a car, and never scores a gap as a plus. But that was where it stopped: I got a list of gaps and no way to close them. The most valuable thing it knew about a car — what it did not know — led nowhere. The new module turns those gaps into questions for the seller and things to check, each with a reason and a source. Tick-boxes, downloadable to print. The Recommendation page used to build a similar list with its own logic; it now uses the same code, because two versions of the same list would drift apart and nobody would know which one held. The other half of the release is a fix I would not have found on my own. I sent myself a photo of a real VIN etched on glass — and the module, with thirty green tests and flawless reading of rendered plates, read nothing at all. I expected trouble with light text on a dark background; measurement showed it was size: upscaled to 1600 pixels nothing, at 2400 flawless. Preprocessing is now a ladder of variants that stops at the first one that works — and the result says which one it was. It is exactly the lesson the app has written down next to its other bugs: green tests are no proof that it works. 241 tests.
  • v0.5.0 — VIN from a photo (September 2026) — the last item from the brief. The module does not read the VIN; it proposes candidates that a person must confirm. That difference is not cosmetic: a misread VIN is worse than none, because the history check would then run on someone else’s car and look documented. So the app never saves a VIN by itself, and the raw text the OCR produced is always shown next to the proposals. Recognition uses the OCR built into Windows — free, offline, nothing extra to install. The interesting part was that reading was the smaller half of the job. The bigger half was stopping the module from making VINs up. Four cases turned up where it “found” a VIN in perfectly ordinary text — in a bank account number, an invoice line, in “STK 06/2027 KM 182350” and in “posledni servis 03/2025”. All of them are seventeen characters too, and a random sequence matches the check digit about once in eleven tries. Every such find became a rule and a test; trust now depends on where the sequence came from — a standalone word, a group of words joined together, or a slice of a longer number. Along the way it turned out that Windows OCR reads a zero on a darker plate as “e” and elsewhere as “ô”, which is now in the substitution table. 219 tests. What is not verified yet: it has been tried on rendered plates, not on photos of real cars — reading a generated image does not prove it can read a stamping on a body panel.
  • v0.4.0 — selling a car, and the bug that meant the app never updated itself (September 2026) — a new Sell my car module: I record my own vehicle and it gets a valuation from the offers the app knows. What matters is what the valuation refuses to do: it issues no trade-in estimate (I have no documented trade-in prices), it does not claim “this is what it will sell for” (I only know asking prices, and a listing that disappears proves no sale), and it does not adjust for equipment or colour, because I have no basis for how much — an invented percentage would be worse than none. When there are too few comparable offers, the result is “weak sample” or “not enough data”, not a confident number. Every valuation is a version with a data snapshot, so the calculation can be repeated at any time. Then the mandatory update test uncovered something worse than a missing feature: automatic updates had never worked, from the very first release. The batch called find without a path, and on my machine Git’s GNU find comes first in PATH and does not know that switch — so the app was never closed before installing and the installer refused to overwrite it. From the outside it just looked as if “nothing happened”. The lesson was worth it: an update must be judged by the installer’s exit code, not by the window reappearing — the batch restarts the app even after a failure. Fixed with absolute paths and a test that stops a PATH lookup from creeping back in. 188 tests.
  • v0.3.0 — pick-lists and automatic VIN checking (September 2026) — two things. First: make, model, generation, colour and home location stopped being free-text fields and became pick-lists (39 makes, 303 models, 79 towns), because a typo in the make quietly broke the market comparison. The catalogue keys deliberately match what the app normalises incoming data to, so a selection lines up with stored data — a test guards it. Second: the VIN check starts on its own; the hook sits at the single point every route into the app passes through, so none can be missed. The queue lives in the database (it survives a restart), one job per VIN, cached per source. The most important part is what must not happen: a timeout or a parser error is never stored as “no record”. The source survey came out sober — automatic are the app's own records and NHTSA vPIC (which decodes the VIN but gives no history); the Czech registries have a CAPTCHA or no public API, so they stayed a manual step. I do not invent endpoints. Along the way four of my own bugs surfaced, among them that the make catalogue never made it into the installer — the pick-lists would have been empty in the installed app and nobody would have known why. The post-build check now also verifies that key screens actually return data, not just that the server came up. 165 tests. The “Sell my car” module is not in this version yet.
  • v0.2.1 — the bug that left the app invisible (September 2026) — the guard that stops a print() from killing the server in windowless mode was tied to the packaged build only. A development launch through pythonw.exe therefore did not have it, and the app kept running as a process with no window and no server — holding a lock that later made the silent install fail. It was found only because I actually ran the launcher after writing it, instead of just writing it.
  • v0.2.0 — first release (September 2026) — the app got an installer, a silent update from my home NAS (with a mandatory checksum check of the downloaded file) and its own repository. The update was verified by actually going through it from the old version to the new one, not by running the installer by hand. The packaged-build test immediately revealed that three data folders had been bundled to the wrong path and the app crashed on start — something ordinary tests would never have found, because in the source tree everything was fine.
  • Follow-ups after the first release (September 2026) — the overall risk level stopped labelling every car “medium” because of a single missing field (a missing value is a gap, not a warning sign); an offline distance estimate was added, verified against ten known routes with a deviation under 12 %; the knowledge base of model weak spots grew from 10 to 27 entries — mostly the platform siblings of the Trafic, which are in fact the same vehicles under a different badge; and a CSV template for manual import was added.
  • v0.1.0 — the core (September 2026) — normalisation, de-duplication by VIN and similarity, market comparison with a median and a mileage correction, a deterministic score across six areas, risk rules, a watchlist, price history and a Health Center. From the start the rule was that the agent never writes to the database directly — everything goes through a validated contract, so no unchecked value can get into the records.
